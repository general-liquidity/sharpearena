\subsection{Sealed evaluation seeds: defeating the bounded-band scanner}\label{sec:sealed-seeds}

The public held-out set is disjoint from training but enumerable. The band scanner of \cref{sec:predictability} therefore recovers its scenario identities without violating the observation interface. The opt-in mitigation derives each named slot from a secret salt $k$:
\[
\mathrm{seed}_i=\texttt{EVAL\_SEED\_BASE}+
\bigl(F(k,i)\bmod(2^{64}-\texttt{EVAL\_SEED\_BASE})\bigr).
\]
The shipped $F$ combines FNV-1a/64 with public SplitMix64 finalizers. It is deterministic and pinned across Rust and Python, and the tested salts and slots produce held-out-band seeds distinct from the public set. No universal injectivity claim is possible for a finite 64-bit mapping, and this construction is PRF-style rather than a certified MAC.

The intended workflow is commit--reveal. Before evaluation, the host publishes SHA-256$(k)$ and withholds $k$; after scoring, it reveals $k$ so anyone can recompute the named seeds and replay the run. SharpeBench's forward-window format can now bind that hash before entries arrive. This paper's evidence is a simulated workflow containing the revealed salt, not proof that a live evaluation used secret custody, a non-reuse policy or a reveal deadline; the already-open companion window does not claim sealed evaluation.

On 16 tested slots, the scanner over the old public $2^{16}$ band recovers 16/16 public seeds and 0/16 sealed seeds while the salt is withheld; revealing the salt replays 16/16. Under a uniform-seed approximation, that scanner covers about $3.6\times10^{-15}$ of the derived band. The result is therefore narrow: high-entropy salt secrecy defeats this particular bounded-band enumeration budget. It is not a cryptographic security proof, and it does not answer tape-level inversion after observations begin.
